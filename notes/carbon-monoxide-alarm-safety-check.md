A carbon monoxide alarm is easy to shop for as if it were another smart-home accessory. Product pages emphasize displays, voice prompts, mobile alerts, connectivity, and appearance. Those features can help, but they answer a secondary question. The first question is whether the exact alarm can perform its primary safety job in the home where it will be installed.

I work as a Sr. Services Engineer at Viyu Network Solutions. I developed this guide with agent-assisted research and checked its factual core against the public safety and certification sources linked below. It does not describe or imply any Viyu product, system, customer, architecture, control, vendor relationship, workflow, evaluation method, or operating practice.

The practical question is: **what should a homeowner verify before comparing carbon monoxide alarm features?** My answer is to check the exact model's certification and intended application, audible alarm behavior, power and backup, placement and interconnection fit, maintenance and replacement instructions, and recall status. Smart features come after those checks, not before them.

## A current recall shows why the model number matters

On November 25, 2026, the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission announced a recall of Steadfast Haven KH158 and GC03 plug-in carbon monoxide and natural-gas detectors because they can fail to alert consumers to hazardous carbon monoxide. The model numbers and identifying label details appear in the [official CPSC notice](https://www.cpsc.gov/Recalls/2027/Zenara-Recalls-4-in-1-Plug-in-Steadfast-Haven-Carbon-Monoxide-CO-and-Natural-Gas-Detectors-Due-to-Risk-of-Serious-Injury-or-Death-from-Failure-to-Alert-Consumers-to-Hazardous-Levels-of-Gas-and-CO).

At the time of review, the CPSC notice and the [recalling firm's page](https://truststeadfast.com/pages/recall) agreed on the affected models and hazard but gave conflicting interim-use directions. The CPSC notice said to continue using a recalled detector until its replacement was installed; the firm's page said to stop using it immediately. This article does not choose between safety-critical instructions that may change. An owner of either model should use the contact routes on those live recall pages to obtain current instructions. If an alarm sounds or anyone suspects carbon monoxide, follow emergency guidance instead of waiting for a recall response.

The conflict reinforces a broader rule: a product name, storefront badge, or feature list is not the product's full identity. Certification records, instructions, and recalls can apply to one model without applying to a similarly named device. Photograph the label of an installed alarm. Before buying, obtain the exact manufacturer and model from the device, package, or manual; stop if the seller will not provide enough information to verify it.

That discipline also helps with connected products. My [Matter smart-home purchase checklist](/notes/matter-smart-home-purchase-checklist) separates baseline compatibility from optional features, while the [supplier-verification guide](/notes/top-search-result-supplier-verification) explains why a prominent search result cannot verify the seller or offer by itself.

## What does certification prove?

The [CPSC carbon monoxide fact sheet](https://www.cpsc.gov/safety-education/safety-guides/carbon-monoxide/carbon-monoxide-fact-sheet) recommends a CO alarm that meets the requirements of the current UL 2034 safety standard. It also calls an alarm added protection rather than a substitute for correct use and upkeep of appliances that can produce carbon monoxide.

A certification logo is not enough by itself. Match the manufacturer and exact model to a certification record, confirm that the record covers a carbon monoxide alarm for the intended application, and compare the mark and identifying information on the product with the record.

UL identifies single- and multiple-station carbon monoxide alarms under product category CZHF and says those products are investigated to UL 2034. Combination smoke-and-CO alarms must satisfy the applicable requirements for both functions; certification of one function should not be inferred from the other. UL's [CO alarm guidance](https://www.ul.com/thecodeauthority/knowledge/carbon-monoxide-alarm-codes) explains the categories, and its [Product iQ page](https://www.ul.com/software/product-sourcing-and-certifications-database) points to the certification database.

Other recognized laboratories use their own certification marks and scopes. OSHA's [Nationally Recognized Testing Laboratory FAQ](https://www.osha.gov/nationally-recognized-testing-laboratory-program/frequently-asked-questions) is useful for understanding that a mark is tied to a testing body, an applicable standard, and a recognized scope. OSHA's authority in that program concerns workplace requirements, not a general federal purchasing rule for a homeowner. Residential requirements still come from applicable state and local rules.

Certification is evidence about a defined safety function and application. It is not an endorsement of the seller, a promise of future support, proof that an app will remain available, or evidence that the exact model has no recall. Keep those claims separate.

## Put the primary alarm before secondary notifications

UL describes the CO signal for alarms in its UL 2034 discussion as four spaced tones followed by a silent period, distinct from the three-tone smoke-alarm pattern. The exact product instructions should explain its alarm, low-battery, trouble, and end-of-life signals. Before purchase, confirm that household members can hear or otherwise perceive the primary warning and distinguish it from other alerts.

CPSC cautions that the manual test button checks the circuitry, not the accuracy of the carbon monoxide sensor. Testing still matters, but a successful button test does not turn an aging sensor into a verified one or replace the manufacturer's end-of-life and replacement instructions.

Mobile notifications, voice prompts, displays, and automations can add useful context. Evaluate them as additions to the primary local warning, not substitutes for verified alarm behavior, power, installation, and maintenance. If an app or account matters to the purchase, check its privacy terms, update path, support period, and behavior during an internet outage separately.

## Match power, interconnection, and placement to the home

CPSC recommends battery-operated CO alarms or alarms with battery backup on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas. Its fact sheet says hardwired or plug-in alarms should have battery backup and describes interconnected alarms as preferable because activation of one causes all connected alarms to sound.

The useful buying question is not simply "battery or plug-in?" It is whether the documented power arrangement supports the alarm during the conditions the household needs to plan for. Check the backup type, low-power signal, approved replacement battery when applicable, and behavior when external power is lost.

Interconnection requires an exact compatibility check. Do not assume that two alarms interconnect because they share a brand, app, or wireless technology. Confirm supported models and installation methods in the manufacturer instructions. For new construction or a required installation, check the design against local code.

Plan placement before quantity. The [U.S. Fire Administration](https://www.usfa.fema.gov/prevention/life-safety-hazards/carbon-monoxide/index.html) advises installing CO alarms in a central location outside each separate sleeping area and on every level of the home. CPSC gives the same general coverage advice and directs consumers to follow manufacturer instructions. CPSC also says to avoid locations near heating vents, places where furniture or draperies can cover an alarm, kitchens, and positions above fuel-burning appliances.

Count the required locations first, then decide whether single-station or interconnected alarms fit. One feature-rich device cannot compensate for leaving needed areas uncovered.

## Treat maintenance and recall checks as product features

Find the manual before buying and answer four questions: How often should the alarm be tested? How is backup power maintained? What do its low-battery, trouble, and end-of-life signals mean? When must the complete unit be replaced?

The [CPSC CO Alarms center](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Carbon-Monoxide-Information-Center/CO-Alarms) recommends monthly tests. The fact sheet directs consumers to the manufacturer's replacement age and explains that newer alarms provide an end-of-life signal. Record the installation and replacement dates somewhere the household will retain.

Search the exact manufacturer and model in the [CPSC recall index](https://www.cpsc.gov/Recalls) before purchase and periodically after installation. A result requires model-level reading: match the label, affected variants, hazard, and current official instructions. A retailer comment, social post, or similarly named listing cannot replace the regulator's notice.

The useful maintenance record is small: model, installation date, power source, test schedule, replacement or end-of-life date, and the recall page checked. That record is more valuable than a feature comparison once the alarm is on the wall.

## Use a five-minute pre-purchase check

1. Record the exact manufacturer, model number, and intended application from the label, package, or manual.
2. Match that model to the claimed certification directory and confirm the relevant carbon monoxide alarm category or standard.
3. Check the primary warning, test control, power backup, trouble signals, and end-of-life behavior.
4. Map the required locations and verify interconnection against manufacturer instructions and local requirements.
5. Search current recalls, then save the manual, receipt, installation date, and planned replacement date.

There is no universal winner. A suitable choice depends on the installation, local requirements, household hearing and accessibility needs, available power, interconnection plan, and willingness to maintain the device. The defensible sequence is universal: verify the safety baseline first, then compare convenience.

This guide does not replace local code, a qualified installer's judgment, or product instructions. Travel, recreational-vehicle, marine, occupational, and system-connected applications can have different listings and requirements. Match the alarm to its actual use instead of extending a residential product claim into another setting.

If a CO alarm sounds, do not investigate the source before getting clear. CPSC says to move outside to fresh air immediately, call emergency services or 911, account for everyone, and not reenter until responders give permission. Follow the emergency instructions supplied with the alarm and the directions of local responders.
